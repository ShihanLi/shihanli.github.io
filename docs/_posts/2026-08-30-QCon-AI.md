In June, I attended the QCon AI conference by InfoQ. It was a software development conference for engineers focusing on emerging trends and engineering practices. It was an incredible learning experience talking to engineers and leaders across companies to understand their AI strategies.

My top takeaways:
- Engineering roles are turning into agent managers the more AI mature a company is.
    - Humans set intention, agents run loops.
- Bottlenecks prevent organizations from achieving real productivity gains from AI (theory of constraints).
- Costs are a growing concern. 
    - International firms are ahead in cost savings due to higher cost of tokens.
    - Biggest savings come from centralized infra changes.

## Agentic Engineering

Martin Spiers, who leads the performance team at OpenAI, talked about how his team uses agents to keep up with the rapidly growing user base and the speed of development from engineering teams.

Their workflow contains 3 levels of feedback loops. 

For every PR, an agent loop runs micro-benchmarks on their critical paths. If there's a regression in latency, memory allocation, or bundle sizes (slows down initial load on mobile), it profiles the diffs, implements fixes, and re-runs the benchmarks.

For each rollout, an agent deploys the candidate change to canary, consisting of a small percentage of real traffic. It monitors regressions visible only with real traffic, p95/p99 latency, time to first token, token stream interruptions, backpressure, etc. It can pause rollout, compare traces and profiles from before and after, propose and validate a fix, and continue rollout. The awareness of "change information", content of code deployed and when, helps improve the accuracy of the agent's reasoning.

The last level is always on, where different agents look at code, traces, logs, and can ssh into production servers to run profiles to find opportunities to optimize or make fixes.

In conversations with Martin after the talk, I learned that each engineer on his team usually works on 7-10 things at a time. They think of feedback loops as safety nets, believing in their capability to catch any errors in code and autonomously fix or rollback, which makes them comfortable with the increasing rate of change. Their strategy has been to connect agents with more and more "signals" 

## Measuring Productivity

Lizzie Matusov, the co-founder and CEO of Quotient, researches why organizations have struggled with translating AI expenditures to real productivity gains. 

Often times, companies use activity metrics that are easy to measure but does not translate directly to business impact. These include tokens burned, licenses issued, suggestions accepted, number of PRs per engineer...

She recommends measuring productivity through speed, quality, and ease. How fast are features delivered to customers, how much is customer trust and satisfaction in the product, and how efficient and friction free is the product development process. 

Once companies recalibrate their metrics, she recommends relentlessly focusing on improving the biggest bottleneck to see real improvements in productivity. Every system is limited by a single bottleneck, speeding up anything else does not increase output, but rather piles work in front of the slow step.

Lastly, there are 5 stages of AI maturity. Companies make the mistake of having unrealistic expectations or jumping over stages without having foundations built from previous stages, leading to negative impact on business outcomes.
- Ad hoc experimentation: devs explore tools independently, and the org has limited visibility
- Assisted development: devs use AI to assist with coding and docs, but usage patterns remain inconsistent across teams. Early adopters and skeptics bifurcate. Harnesses around our code are not built to handle the throughput
- Standardized workflows: AI is embedded into development workflows and supported by shared organizational practices. Automated validation checks begin verifying AI generated work, and teams adopt consistent expectations for how AI is used. 
- Supervised automation: AI agents begin executing bounded engineering tasks automatically. Agents handle lower complexity work while engineers oversee higher risk decisions
- End to end autonomy: AI orchestrates multi step engineering workflows with minimal human intervention. Engineers shift towards supervising systems and managing exceptions. 

## Costs

Token cost was a big topic of discussion at the conference.

The majority of companies I spoke with are in the early stages of exploring token saving strategies.

Usually there is a central infra team that assigns a fixed budget per engineer per period. The engineer can request more if they run over. The requests get automatically approved, but it brings cost awareness to the forefront, and prevents runaway costs from agents.

In contrast, international companies started cost lowering work years ago. Token costs abroad are more expensive due to currency conversion rates, and some languages taking more tokens to express the same ideas in English.

Totvs, Brazil's largest tech company, has focused on optimizing the infrastructure around MCP servers and tools, which is the primary way agents interact with services. 

Precise tools are better than generic tools.

Scoping down the `execute_query(prompt)` tool in Kusto MCP into `get_compliance_state(device)` and `get_report_for_setting(setting)` allows the tool to return less useless data, and push logic from the prompt into the tool. The saved context lowers costs, and avoids pushing agent over the smart context, which is still around 100k tokens.

Just in time tool search prevents context bloat as number of tools grows.

At session start, the harness typically connects to the configured MCP servers, calls the tool-list tool, receives the name, description, and argument schema for each tool, and injects them into session context alongside the conversation. In Intune, the available MCP tools take up 2/3 of the smart context.

At Totvs, they created a search tool service, where agents can find tool definitions with semantic search, and get back 5 candidates that most fit the request. The token growth as number of tools increases is linear, whereas with dynamic tool search it's constant. 

## Evals

Evals are to agents what tests are for code. 

In the context of Copilot CLI, the agent we primarily interact with, an example eval could be whether it called the correct tool when needed, or if invoking the /icm-triage skill reported findings in the format specified. 

They are extremely custom to scenarios. Generic evals in the best case waste the company's time and resources, in the worst case, lead to a false sense of complacency. So every company that makes an AI product needs to create their own evals and associated infrastructure. 

Because the product I work on is not an AI product, evals mostly do not apply to our business logic. Even though our customers will be using AI agents to interact with our product, the agents will just use Microsoft Graph (API platform for M365 services) rather than a natural language interface.

But evals will become important as more and more of our internal processes become agentified, and we need a way to ensure they run reliably. Skills, for one. Some skills investigate scenarios, some skills create automation workflows. Without evals, we have fewer insights into how well they work. 

There are three stages of creating evals. 

First is the eye test. You make a skill, run it, and see whether it works or not. 

Second is to write down the key scenarios the skill should handle well. In the example of investigating a feature scenario: did it use all the Kusto queries that cover all components of the scenario, did it diagnose the root cause correctly upon seeing a Kusto logline, did the generated report contain raw loglines that back up the analysis as specified in the instructions. 

Once you have decent coverage of key paths, you can publish the skill and collect real traces. You can iteratively add to the evals whenever it didn't handle something correctly. Copy down the real traces and session context as a snapshot, reconstruct it in an eval, modify the wording of your skill (provide good examples) or introduce more deterministic components, or finetune the model to make the eval pass. 

Rinse and repeat. 

## Overheard around the room
1. Agents will decide what infrastructure or product to use. Important for companies to make their product easy to use for agents.
2. Despite the growth in model context, the "smart" or practical token limit is still around 100k. After which model performance begins to degrade.
3. Evals for engineers = output / (salary + token use)
4. Time tested engineering principles are more important today. Enforce small/stacked PRs, 200 lines of business logic, to make it easier for reviews.
5. Engineers mention burnout from using AI, which comes from pressure to work longer hours to meet accelerated timelines, mentally exhausting reading AI generated text, and constantly needing to change workflows to adapt to new capabilities. Leaders told me people get used to working with AI and multi tasking.
